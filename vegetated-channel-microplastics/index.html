Research project on microplastics in vegetated channels

The forces on every microplastic, at the speed of a GPU.

A GPU accelerated Eulerian-Lagrangian model for microplastics transport in water systems

Danial Goodarzi, Abdolmajid Mohammadian, Abolghasem Pilechi and Soroush Abolfathi

Explore the model → See the exponential integrator

A schematic channel with submerged vegetation, in which the microplastics are carried by the slow flow within the stems and the faster flow above them, rise to the surface or settle to the bed.

I

Between tracer models and particle-resolved simulations

Aquatic vegetation modifies the velocity distribution in open channels and affects the longitudinal dispersion and the fate of microplastics, whose transport also depends on their size, density and shape. Existing models either treat microplastics as dissolved tracers with a prescribed settling velocity, or resolve the forces on individual particles with methods such as CFD-DEM, at a computational cost that restricts them to small domains and short durations.

This study develops a GPU accelerated Eulerian-Lagrangian model that occupies the middle ground. The flow in vegetated and non-vegetated channels is computed in OpenFOAM, and every microplastic is then tracked on a graphics processing unit with its own force balance, including drag, buoyancy, added mass, shear induced lift and turbulent dispersion, together with deposition at the bed and trapping at the free surface. An exponential drag integrator removes the time step limit that the short relaxation time of microplastics imposes on conventional particle solvers.

II

The journey of one microplastic

1

Release upstream

The microplastic is released near the free surface at the upstream end of the flume. From this moment, its velocity is calculated from the force balance at each time step with the exponential update, and its position changes by advection and by a random displacement that represents the turbulence.

2

Carried by the overflow layer

Above the stems the flow is fast, and the drag carries the particle downstream at nearly the velocity of the surrounding water, while the random displacement, scaled by the eddy diffusivity, moves it up and down through the overflow layer.

3

Through the shear layer

At the top of the vegetation the velocity changes sharply, and the large eddies of this shear layer exchange water and particles between the overflow and the stems. The eddy diffusivity reaches its maximum here, and the Saffman lift acts across the shear.

4

Among the stems

Within the vegetation the flow is slow, and the particle travels through the wakes between the stems, where the drift correction prevents an artificial accumulation in the regions of low diffusivity. The difference between this slow layer and the overflow stretches the cloud of microplastics along the channel.

5

Counted at a virtual station

When the particle crosses a virtual station, placed at the location of a laboratory fluorometer, its crossing time is recorded. The crossings of all particles give the response curve, from which the longitudinal dispersion coefficient is calculated with the method of moments.

6

Settling to the bed

Being denser than the water, the particle settles toward the end of its path. At the bed it is deposited while the bed shear velocity remains below the critical value of the Shields criterion, and it is re-entrained when the bed shear velocity exceeds this value.

III

From the flow field to every particle

The flow is computed once, mapped once onto a structured grid on the GPU, and the particles are then tracked through this frozen flow field, which is appropriate for the dilute suspensions of microplastics in water systems.

Each particle finds its local fluid values in a structured grid that is stretched toward the bed and the side walls, so that the search for the hosting cell is replaced by a closed form index and a single read of the stored fields. The fluid fields and the complete particle state remain in the memory of the GPU for the whole simulation.

  • The flow is computed once in OpenFOAM and held fixed during particle tracking.
  • Vegetation enters the flow as a distributed drag force and as an additional source of turbulence dissipation from the stem wakes.
  • The virtual stations are placed at the locations of the laboratory fluorometers, and the dispersion coefficients are calculated with the same method of moments as the measurements.
IV

The force balance on one microplastic

Each microplastic is a rigid particle with its own density, volume and shape, accelerated by the sum of the forces that act on it, and moved by the turbulence through a random displacement.

The particle velocity changes with the drag force, the body force of gravity and buoyancy, and the Saffman lift in the sheared flow near the bed and at the top of the vegetation. The added mass of the surrounding water is included in the inertia of the particle.

Because a RANS flow field provides only the mean velocity, the turbulent velocity fluctuations are represented by a random displacement scaled by the eddy diffusivity, and a drift correction removes the artificial accumulation of particles in regions of low diffusivity near the walls and within the vegetation.

  • Drag of spheres from the Schiller-Naumann law, and of other shapes from a shape-dependent drag.
  • A drag coefficient that depends on the turbulence, for particles of millimetre size.
  • Each sub-model verified against an analytical solution.
V

An exponential drag integrator

A microplastic adjusts to the surrounding flow in a fraction of a millisecond, and an explicit integrator must resolve this relaxation time to remain stable. The exponential update treats the linearized drag exactly, so the time step is set by the flow rather than by the particle.

Over each time step, the drag coefficient and the forces other than drag are held constant, and the velocity equation is then solved analytically. The update is exact for the linearized drag and unconditionally stable, so the particles can be advanced with time steps many times larger than their relaxation time, while the trapezoidal position update keeps the advection consistent with the old and new velocities.

vn+1 = u + (vn − u) e−Δt/τ + (F/m) τ (1 − e−Δt/τ)

VI

Vegetation and the dispersion of microplastics

Submerged vegetation divides the flow into a slow layer within the stems and a faster layer above them, and the exchange between the two layers spreads the microplastics along the channel.

The stems are not resolved individually. Their effect on the flow is applied through a drag force in the cells occupied by vegetation, proportional to the stem drag coefficient and the frontal area of the stems per unit volume, and through an additional dissipation of turbulence generated by the stem wakes. This representation captures the momentum loss and the shear layer at the top of the vegetation that control dispersion at the scale of the channel, at a cost that allows many configurations of vegetation and discharge to be simulated.

The model predicts the dispersion from the computed flow field and the force balance on each particle, rather than from measured velocity profiles, so it can be applied to vegetation configurations and discharges for which no measurements are available.

VII

Buoyant and settling microplastics

Microplastics lighter than the water rise toward the surface and microplastics denser than the water settle toward the bed, and three boundary rules decide what happens when they arrive.

VIII

Particles of different shapes

Microplastics in water systems occur as spheres, fibres, flat fragments and irregular grains, and their drag depends on the shape and on the orientation during settling.

For a non-spherical particle, the drag is calculated from the projected area of its broadest face and from a drag coefficient that depends on the shape, while the mass, the added mass and the body force follow from the particle volume. The settling velocity is therefore obtained from the three dimensions of the particle, without calibration.

For particles of millimetre size, the settling velocity in a turbulent flow differs from the settling velocity in still water, and the model provides a drag coefficient that increases with the ratio of the bed shear velocity to the settling velocity for this range of particle sizes.

IX

Tested against laboratory experiments

After verification of each sub-model against analytical solutions, the coupled model is compared without calibration with four groups of laboratory measurements.
