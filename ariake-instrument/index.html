Ariake Sea microplastic survey instrument

Place a few stations, enter what you measured, and read the whole bay: the suspended field with its uncertainty, the governing process, its parameters, and where to sample next.

Trained on - simulated years of the bay. It has never been tested against a real Ariake sample. Bundle built -.

How this works, in four sentences

Every year the bay could plausibly have is a mix of fifteen basic patterns, learned from the simulated campaign. Your readings do not have to determine thousands of cell values, only those fifteen numbers, which is why a handful of samples is enough. The calculation is done three times, once for each process, and how well each version explains your readings becomes the probability that this process is governing. The same formula that gives the best estimate also gives the spread around it, which is the uncertainty map.

Click the water to place a station. Amber marks the next station the instrument would choose.

The map. Blue is a low concentration, red is a high one. The dashed part of the coastline is the open sea boundary of the model, the solid part is land. Clicking anywhere on the water adds a station and a row in the table beside it. Click the small cross in a row to remove one.

Where a station would help. Tick it and every candidate cell is coloured by how much a single reading there would reduce the uncertainty of the whole field. Before you enter anything it shows the best places to plan a cruise. After you press "Read the bay" it updates to show where the next station should go, given what you already know.

Hover anywhere on any map to read the value and the uncertainty at that point.

Release points and attribution. The model releases plastic at fourteen points around the bay. Tick "release points" to see them. Tick "how attributable" and every cell is coloured by how strongly a single one of them dominates the plastic there: red where the origin is nearly certain, blue where several rivers mix. Hovering names the largest source, says how often it leads across the weighted scenarios, and gives the age of that plastic. This layer comes from a separate study of source attribution, built on 610 simulations of the same bay, and loads the first time you ask for it.

Age, and whether two sources can be told apart. The source study tracks how long each particle has been in the water. That is what makes attribution work: plastic from a distant river arrives older than plastic from a nearby one. Two sources can only be separated when their transit times to your station differ, and the campaign measured the limit at about one day. The station panel reports the age and says whether your top two sources clear it.

The backdrop. With "campaign average" ticked you see the typical field for the month chosen below the reconstructed map, before any of your readings are used. It is what the instrument believes before you tell it anything.

Your survey

Type the suspended microplastic concentration you measured at each station, in whatever unit your laboratory reported, for example pieces per cubic metre. The unit itself does not matter, only that every station uses the same one. The instrument reads the pattern across your stations, and solves for the scale as an unknown.

The measurement error slider. It tells the instrument how much to trust each reading. Move it right and the reconstruction leans more on the campaign and less on you, and the uncertainty widens. The paper's tests show the map barely changes between 0.25 and 2, but the honesty of the uncertainty does, so set it near the truth for your method.

Below the limit. A station where nothing was found is still informative. Tick the small box beside that reading and give the detection limit above. The reading then enters as "somewhere below this line" rather than as a number.

Saving and exporting. "Save survey" writes your stations, readings and settings to a small file you can reopen later or send to a colleague. "Export the map" writes the reconstructed concentration and its uncertainty for every cell of the shown month as a spreadsheet, with the units and the settings recorded at the top. "Print summary" produces a one page record, and the print dialogue of any browser can save it as a PDF.

The worked examples are real scenarios from the campaign that the instrument was never built from, so the truth is known and a third map appears with the correlation printed. Each one demonstrates a different finding of the paper. Load one, then change the months, the error slider or the stations and press "Read the bay" again to see what happens.

What the readings imply

Reconstructed field

The suspended concentration across the whole bay, implied by your readings. If you entered a real unit, this map is in that same unit. Every month of the year can be shown, because the fifteen patterns describe the year jointly. Months you did not sample simply carry more uncertainty.

Uncertainty

Where this is bright, the map beside it is a guess.

A factor, not a concentration. A value of four means the true concentration in that cell is likely to lie between a quarter and four times what the map beside it shows. It is small near your stations and large where the three process priors disagree.

The truth

Only shown for a worked example. This is the field the simulation actually produced, which the instrument never saw. Compare it with the reconstruction beside it, and check that the errors sit where the uncertainty map says they should.

Which process is governing

Three answers to the same question, by different routes. The first two come from trained networks, one naming which process was switched on and one naming what the particles are actually doing. The third is the closed form evidence of the three priors, computed from the same update that draws the map.

The refusal matters. Below nine in ten the instrument does not name a process. That rule is from the paper, where the answers it does commit to are right in ninety three to ninety eight per cent of cases. Silence here is a result, not a failure.

The mathematics, in short

Everything on this page comes from the seven lines below. They are the equations of the paper, written compactly. Bold letters are vectors and matrices.

The field
x = x + Φa

The suspended field over every wet cell and all twelve months is the campaign mean x plus a mix of the 15 patterns Φ. The unknown is a, fifteen numbers, not 6,893 cell values. This is why a handful of readings is enough.

A survey
y = xH + Ha + ε,   ε ~ N(0, R)

H holds the rows of Φ at the cells and months you sampled. R is the reading error you set with the slider, plus the part of the field the fifteen patterns cannot describe.

The update,
once per process
af = μf + Kfrf,   Cf = Σf − KfHΣf
Kf = ΣfHTSf−1,   Sf = HΣfHT + R,   rf = y − xH − Hμf

The standard Gaussian update, run three times: once with the biofouling prior, once with fragmentation, once with fate. μf and Σf are that process's prior over the fifteen numbers, learned from the campaign.

Which process
wf ∝ N(y; xH + Hμf, Sf)

How well each of the three versions explains your readings, normalised over the three. These are the bars in the process panel. Below nine in ten the page names no process.

The map
m = ∑f wf mf,   mf = x + Φaf

The three answers averaged by their own evidence. This is the reconstructed field.

The uncertainty
v = ∑f wf [ diag(ΦCfΦT) + (mf − m)2 ] + ρ

Two sources added together: how unsure each process is about its own answer, and how much the three processes disagree with each other. ρ is the per-cell error of the fifteen-pattern representation. The second map shows 10√v, the factor either way.

Where to sample next
s* = arg maxs (hsTSWS hs) / (hsTS hs + σe2),   W = ΦTΦ

The cell whose reading would most reduce the total uncertainty of the whole field, not the cell that is most uncertain by itself. This is the amber marker, and the rule that produced the printed plan.

Two extensions
H → [H 1],   a → (a, o)
yi → E[ yi | yi < L ]

The first carries an unknown common scale o as one more unknown, which is why any unit can be entered. The second replaces a reading that only says "below the limit L" by the mean of what the model expects under that bound.

In the paper these are Equations 1 to 5, with the two extensions in the section on baselines and further tests. The page solves them exactly, in about fifteen milliseconds, with no approximation beyond the fifteen patterns themselves.

The method is the one published in “A simulation-trained Bayesian instrument for microplastic surveys”. The field, its uncertainty, the evidence weights and the next-station advice use the paper's own equations. The parameter ranges use a simpler route than the paper's neural posteriors and are labelled where they appear.
